2. NUS marine scientists "HIIT-train" Singapore's corals with heat conditioning and beneficial bacteria

NUS Lee Kong Chian Natural History Museum associate professor Huang Danwei and her team have transplanted 160 coral fragments onto artificial reefs off Kusu Island and St John's Island, exposing some to short bursts of elevated water temperature — dubbed "heat HIIT" — and to potentially beneficial bacteria strains, to see whether pre-conditioning can raise corals' tolerance for marine heatwaves. Researchers will revisit the reefs biweekly over a year, tracking growth, survival, colour and photosynthetic efficiency through the warmest months of 2026, a period compounded by a forecast El Niño. The project positions NUS at the centre of Singapore's practical response to reef bleaching risk, translating lab-scale probiotic and thermal-tolerance research into a real-world conservation trial. It builds on the university's long-running coral reef ecology programme and adds a hands-on, positive-sentiment science story to a week otherwise dominated by policy commentary.

Sources: Straits Times, Red Hot Singapore

4. NUS geographer Gretchen Coffman champions a "roots to reefs" approach to mangrove conservation

Marking World Mangrove Day (26 July), NUS Faculty of Arts and Social Sciences Senior Lecturer Dr Gretchen Coffman was profiled by the university's own newsroom on her "roots to reefs" teaching philosophy, which treats mangroves, seagrass meadows and coral reefs as one connected system rather than isolated habitats. She noted that under 1% of Singapore's land area remains mangrove habitat versus roughly 13% of the original coastline, yet the country still hosts 35 of the world's roughly 83 true mangrove species and has become a regional conservation leader through sites like Sungei Buloh. The piece highlights her field-based teaching — drone and 3D-mapping expeditions across Southeast Asia, immersive 360-degree conservation films, and hands-on restoration work with NUS students in Indonesia and Malaysia — as evidence of NUS cultivating a next generation of conservation practitioners. It is a purely positive, institution-authored feature that reinforces NUS's public image as a regional environmental-education leader.

Sources: NUS News
